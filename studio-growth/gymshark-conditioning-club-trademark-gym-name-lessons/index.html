Gymshark Is Challenging a Small Gym's Trademark. Here's How to Check Yours.

A Gloucestershire gym registered The Conditioning Club five years ago. Gymshark now wants the name and says it's too generic for anyone to own. The two weak spots it found are ones most gyms and studios share.

Since the gym registered its name

Gymshark is trying to take the name of a small gym in England. The gym registered it first.

The gym is The Conditioning Club, on an industrial estate in Fairford, Gloucestershire. Owner John Watson has about 500 members, nine staff and three freelance trainers. He registered the name with the UK Intellectual Property Office five years ago, for gym and fitness services. The Guardian broke the story on Sunday.

In March 2025, Gymshark applied to register Conditioning Club and The Conditioning Club for clothing, retail and entertainment. Watson objected. Gymshark then asked the office to cancel his original registration. Its argument is that the name is too generic for anyone to own.

Watson's legal bill is heading toward £30,000. A crowdfunding page has raised just over £5,000. Gymshark declined to comment.

You don't need a fight with a £1 billion brand for this to matter. The two weak spots Gymshark found are ones most gyms and studios share.

Why does Gymshark want the name?

It already puts the name on a workout. Fitness First's UK clubs run a 60-minute class called Gymshark Conditioning Club, which the chain says it created with Gymshark. That makes the name part of a class format Gymshark places inside other people's gyms. Gymshark hasn't said publicly why it filed, so take that as context, not motive.

Weak spot one: a name that says what you do

Gymshark's case is that The Conditioning Club is descriptive (a name that simply describes the service). Trademark offices are wary of names like that. Registering one could stop competitors from describing their own classes. A conditioning club that sells conditioning is the textbook example.

Watson's answer is five years of use. Members, staff, and ties with local schools and sports clubs, all built around the name. That can count. A plain name can sometimes be protected once people link it to one business. But proving it takes money and time, and that's what a small owner has least of.

There's an awkward detail on Gymshark's side. It holds several registrations for Lifting Club, which is just as plain.

"I thought I had done what I needed to do to protect us." John Watson, The Conditioning Club

Weak spot two: the merch your registration misses

Trademarks are registered by category. The UK and the US both use the same international list of 45 classes. Fitness services sit in one. Clothing sits in another. Retail sits in a third.

Watson sold branded gear for years, and his registration covered one of the categories Gymshark later filed in. It didn't cover others, like retail. He's now applying to add them, after a much bigger company got there first.

Most studios sell something with their name on it. Tees, hoodies, water bottles, a retreat, an online program. Each can land in a category your registration doesn't reach. A name carries real weight, as we saw when Solidcore dropped the word Pilates. And legal fights get expensive fast, as SLT's suit against Lagree shows.

What this means for you

This applies to a 24-hour gym and a boutique studio alike. Three checks, and none of them needs a lawyer to start.

Is your name a description? If it's the service plus "club", "studio" or "lab", a bigger brand can argue nobody owns it. You can't easily rename a business you've built. But for a new site, program or class, pick something distinctive. Made-up words and odd pairings are the strongest.

Does your registration cover what you sell? Find your certificate and list its classes. Then list everything with your name on it. Search the UK register or the US trademark search to see who else has filed. Adding categories is cheaper before someone else files in them.

Know what a fight costs. Watson's bill is close to £30,000, and his case hasn't reached a court. Some business policies include legal expenses cover. Check yours before you need it, and talk to a trademark attorney before you file anything.

Your name is probably the oldest asset you own. It's also the one you've likely checked least, and it's worth more than the merch it's printed on. As Lululemon showed, the brand is what members come back for.

Gymshark started in a garage. The Conditioning Club started on an industrial estate. Only one of them can afford a long fight over a name.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Gymshark in a trademark dispute with The Conditioning Club?
In March 2025 Gymshark applied to register Conditioning Club and The Conditioning Club for clothing, retail and entertainment. The Conditioning Club, a gym in Fairford, Gloucestershire, had registered the name for fitness services five years earlier and objected. Gymshark then asked the UK Intellectual Property Office to cancel the gym's registration as too generic. The case is still waiting on a decision.
Can you trademark a descriptive gym name?
It's hard. Trademark offices are wary of names that simply describe the service, like a conditioning club that sells conditioning, because they would stop competitors describing their own classes. A plain name can sometimes be protected once customers link it to one business, but proving that takes evidence, time and legal fees.
What trademark classes should a gym or studio register?
Both the UK and the US use the same international list of 45 classes. Fitness services sit in class 41. If you sell branded clothing, that's class 25, and retail services fall under class 35. List everything you sell with your name on it and check each one is covered. A trademark attorney can confirm the right classes before you file.
